Modulating boilers versus on/off thermostats

Why an on/off room thermostat makes a boiler cycle at a high flow temperature, and how a modulating control — OpenTherm, or a manufacturer’s own bus — asks for a lower flow so the boiler can condense.

Updated 4 November 2026

What modulating means

On an A-rated (ErP energy label) gas boiler, modulation can mean two different things.

The burner modulates when the boiler turns its gas rate up and down to match the heat the circuit will take. A wide modulation range helps it avoid firing at full output for a small load. That is a property of the boiler. See burner modulation rate and flow temperature and power output.

The control modulates when it does more than close a switch. Instead of “on” or “off”, it sends a flow-temperature setpoint: how hot the water leaving the boiler should be. The burner then fires only as hard as it needs to hold that target. Most UK rooms still have the first kind of thermostat, even on a recent condensing boiler.

On/off control

A conventional room thermostat is a switch. When the room is below the setpoint it calls for heat; the boiler fires toward its central-heating maximum — often 70–80 °C. Radiators run hotter than the house needs, the room overshoots, and the thermostat drops out. The boiler then sits off until the room cools, and the cycle repeats. That is short cycling: full heat, then nothing, then full heat again.

High flow water usually means a high return. Once the return is above about 55 °C the boiler stops recovering latent heat from the flue gas, so it is no longer condensing even though the case still says “condensing”.

Modulating control

A modulating control talks to the boiler over OpenTherm or the manufacturer’s own bus. It does not slam the burner to the fascia maximum. It sends a flow-temperature setpoint, and it lowers that setpoint as the room approaches the comfort temperature — or as the weather warms. The boiler then runs lower and longer: cooler water, a longer firing period, and far less on/off cycling.

Why lower flow temperature matters

A condensing boiler recovers extra heat when water vapour in the flue gas turns back to liquid on a cool heat-exchanger surface. For natural gas that needs the return water below about 55 °C. Lower is better. The exact dew point is model-dependent; 55 °C is the usual rule of thumb, not a laboratory certificate.

Flow and return move together. If the control keeps asking for 75 °C flow, the return will sit well above the dew point on a typical radiator circuit. If it asks for 45–50 °C, the return can stay in the condensing band for most of the heating season. The burner still modulates; it just does that work at a useful water temperature. More on that relationship is on flow temperature and power output and boiler energy efficiency.

Load compensation, weather compensation, or both

Load compensation looks at the room. The further the room is from the setpoint, the higher the flow temperature it asks for; as the room catches up, the flow target falls. It usually replaces the on/off room thermostat and needs no outdoor sensor.

Weather compensation looks outside. An outdoor sensor — or, on some controls, local weather data — sets the flow temperature from a heating curve: colder air, hotter flow. It does not wait for the room to overshoot before it turns the boiler down.

The two work well together. Weather sets the ballpark flow; the room sensor trims it when the house is sunnier, fuller, or better insulated than the curve assumed. OpenTherm and most manufacturer buses can carry either, or both.

ErP energy labelling gives temperature controls a class and a percentage. That percentage is added to the package seasonal space-heating efficiency on the fiche and label. It is not a promise about your gas bill. The house, the radiators, the setpoints and how the system is commissioned decide what you actually use.

Class Typical control Addition to the package label
V Modulating room thermostat (load compensation) with a modulating heater +3%
VI Weather compensator and room sensor with a modulating heater +4%
VII Weather compensator and room sensor with an on/off heater +3.5%
VIII Multi-sensor room-temperature control with a modulating heater +5%

These class figures come from the EU temperature-control table used for the package label, not from measured household savings. See the 2014 Commission communication on regulations 811/2013 and 812/2013 (CELEX 52014XC0703(01)) and the energy-labelling regulation itself (Regulation (EU) No 811/2013).

Boiler Plus in England

When a new gas combi is fitted in an existing home in England, Boiler Plus requires one extra measure on top of an efficient boiler and time-and-temperature control. Load compensation or weather compensation is one of those options — and it is the one that actually changes the flow temperature. The rules now sit in Approved Document L (conservation of fuel and power).

Compatibility

OpenTherm is a shared two-wire protocol: in principle a control from one maker can talk to a boiler from another. In practice the standard has optional features, so you still check the pair. Some boilers take OpenTherm on the main terminals; others need a small interface module. Brand notes for Vaillant OpenTherm and Worcester Bosch OpenTherm are on this site, and there is a list of OpenTherm compatible boilers.

A manufacturer’s own bus — eBUS is the usual example — can expose more of that boiler’s menus, fault codes and extra zones. The trade-off is lock-in: you are then choosing that maker’s controls, not any OpenTherm unit. Neither path is “better” in the abstract. It depends on whether you want a free choice of control later, or deeper access to one brand.

The document that settles it is the boiler’s installation manual, not a compatibility badge on a box. Look the model up on boilermanuals.com and read the control-connection section before you buy a thermostat.

Next steps

If the boiler already condenses when you turn the heating flow down, a modulating control keeps it there automatically instead of asking you to pick one number for the whole winter. If the radiators will not heat the house at a lower flow, that is a sizing, balancing or insulation problem — not a reason to stay on an on/off high-temperature loop forever.
