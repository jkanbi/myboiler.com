Boiler Optimiser

Lowering your flow temperature is the easiest way to cut your gas use and lower energy bills. It can also help reduce boiler wear and tear over time. Select your system type below, then use the slider to estimate your savings.

A non-condensing boiler cannot be optimised in the same way as a condensing boiler. Any saving from a lower flow temperature is much smaller than on a modern condensing boiler.

  • Dropping the flow from about 80°C to 70°C may save a little.
  • Taking it toward 60°C or below can become risky, depending on the boiler and the system.

What still helps:

  • A room thermostat and a timer, so the boiler is off when the house does not need heat.
  • Lagging on the pipes, and on the hot water cylinder if you have one.
  • Bleeding and balancing the radiators, so heat reaches every room.

Replacing it is the larger step. A condensing boiler is the usual swap. A heat pump can take its place where the radiators and the house are suitable.

Warning: Condensate pipe must be correctly installed

That white plastic pipe under the boiler is the drain for the water a condensing boiler makes. If it freezes, the boiler usually stops.

Fitted properly

It should slope downhill all the way, with no dips. Better if it stays indoors and goes into a drain. If it has to go outside, keep that bit short. Plastic pipe, not copper.

Size

A thin pipe on an outside wall is the one that freezes. Outside, or in a cold loft or garage, it should be a fatter 32 mm pipe.

Insulation

Any outdoor length needs waterproof lagging, including the bends and the open end. Bare pipe on a cold wall will freeze.

If it freezes

Warm it from the ground with a hot-water bottle or warm water — not boiling, and not a flame. Then press reset on the boiler. Turning the flow back up can get you through a cold night, but you lose the saving. Ask a Gas Safe engineer to lag it properly, or run it indoors.

The bigger saving: fewer repairs

Even if your gas saving looks small, a lower flow temperature is gentler on your boiler. It works less hard, parts can last longer and breakdowns are less likely. Avoiding just one repair can be worth more than the gas you save.

    Load compensation (OpenTherm)

    A load-compensating thermostat, sometimes called a modulating thermostat, talks to the boiler instead of just switching it on and off. As rooms get close to the temperature you have set, it turns the flow temperature down. OpenTherm is one common way of doing this; some boilers use the maker’s own protocol.

    This is usually the easier extra option to set up: often just a compatible thermostat swap, with no outdoor sensor or heating curve. Many boiler makers sell their own load-compensating controls that talk to their boilers over the maker’s own connection; these usually only work fully with that maker’s boilers, so check compatibility before you buy. If your boiler does not support OpenTherm, the maker’s own control is often the simplest match; some also offer OpenTherm. It can also be used with weather compensation.

    A sensible fixed, lower flow temperature is still a good, simple start. That is what the Boiler Optimiser helps you find. If you rent, check with your landlord before you change any controls. See OpenTherm boilers and controls and Vaillant controls.

    Weather compensation

    Weather compensation goes a step further and is a little more advanced. It uses a sensor fitted on an outside wall to turn the boiler’s flow temperature down as the weather gets milder. It works well when it is fitted and set up properly, but for some homes it can complicate things.

    That only works well if the heating curve is set for your home. Many combi boilers never had that sensor fitted, or it is there but not set up.

    For most homes a sensible fixed, lower flow temperature is the simpler route. That is what the Boiler Optimiser helps you find. If a compensation sensor is already fitted and working, keep using it. If you rent, check with your landlord before you change any controls or have a sensor fitted.
